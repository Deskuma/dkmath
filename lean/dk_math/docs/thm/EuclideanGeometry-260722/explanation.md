# EuclideanGeometry-260722 — Explanation

## 1. What is being connected

DkMath already has two mathematical stories that were developed for different reasons.

The first story is CF2D.

```text
a two-component state
square-mass preservation
unit-kernel composition
an additive kernel family
cfcos and cfsin as coordinate observations
finite iteration and return
```

The second story is the classical Gauss–Wantzel classification.

```text
which regular n-gons are constructible by straightedge and compass
```

At first these seem to belong to different subjects. One is an algebraic rotation mechanism. The other is a theorem from classical geometry and number theory.

The missing bridge is the one-step generator.

A regular orbit is generated by repeating one unit kernel. If the kernel has exact order `n`, its first `n` powers produce `n` distinct states and the next power returns to the beginning.

The Gauss–Wantzel theorem does not decide whether such a periodic kernel exists. Over the real trigonometric model, it exists for every positive `n`.

What Gauss–Wantzel decides is whether that one-step generator can be built by a finite sequence of quadratic constructions.

That is the exact point where the two routes meet.

## 2. The pre-geometric CF2D side

CF2D begins with a pair:

$$
z=(x,y).
$$

It observes the two-component square mass:

$$
q_2(z)=x^2+y^2.
$$

A second pair acts as a kernel:

$$
r=(a,b).
$$

The kernel product is:

$$
(a,b)\star(x,y)=(ax-by,ay+bx).
$$

The key identity is:

$$
q_2(r\star z)=q_2(r)q_2(z).
$$

Therefore, when:

$$
q_2(r)=1,
$$

the action preserves the square mass of every state:

$$
q_2(r\star z)=q_2(z).
$$

At this stage, no circle is required. There is only a conserved quadratic boundary and an action that cannot leave it.

DkMath calls such an `r` a `UnitKernel`.

## 3. How cfcos and cfsin arise

A `KernelFamily` assigns a unit kernel to each additive parameter.

Its defining composition law is:

$$
K(t+s)=K(t)\star K(s).
$$

The first and second coordinates are observed as:

$$
\operatorname{cfcos}(t)=K(t).\operatorname{core},
$$

$$
\operatorname{cfsin}(t)=K(t).\operatorname{beam}.
$$

The addition formulas then follow from the kernel product. They are not separately inserted as trigonometric axioms.

The real model later identifies:

$$
K(t)=(\cos t,\sin t).
$$

This identification is useful, but the generic repetition theorem does not depend on it.

## 4. The old scalar theorem that was waiting

The normalized phase layer already contains:

$$
\operatorname{step}(k)=\frac1k.
$$

For positive `k`, it proves:

$$
k\operatorname{step}(k)=1.
$$

This says that `k` equal scalar steps fill one normalized cycle.

But this is only a statement about the parameter. It does not yet say that a state has returned.

The missing connection is:

$$
K(k\cdot t)=K(t)^k.
$$

Once this theorem is proved, the old scalar identity becomes an action theorem.

Let:

$$
t=\frac1k.
$$

Then:

$$
K(t)^k=K(kt)=K(1).
$$

When the normalized full-cycle kernel satisfies:

$$
K(1)=K(0)=1,
$$

we obtain:

$$
K(1/k)^k=1.
$$

This is the first major connection planned by the new branch.

## 5. Why return and exact order are different

Suppose:

$$
r^k=1.
$$

This proves that `k` steps return to the initial state.

It does not prove that `k` is the first return time.

For example, when a kernel already satisfies:

$$
r^2=1,
$$

it also satisfies:

$$
r^4=1.
$$

So a theorem saying only `r^4 = 1` cannot distinguish a half-turn generator from a genuine quarter-turn generator.

The exact-order condition is:

$$
r^k=1,
$$

and for every integer `m` with:

$$
0<m<k,
$$

we have:

$$
r^m\ne1.
$$

This minimality condition is what guarantees that the first `k` orbit states are distinct.

The repository already contains a complete exact-order-four prototype. The new project generalizes that idea.

## 6. A regular polygon before drawing edges

Choose a unit kernel `r`, a base state `z`, and define:

$$
v_j=r^j\star z.
$$

Every vertex has the same square mass:

$$
q_2(v_j)=q_2(z).
$$

The successor law is:

$$
v_{j+1}=r\star v_j.
$$

When:

$$
r^k=1,
$$

the orbit is periodic:

$$
v_{j+k}=v_j.
$$

When the order is exactly `k`, the states:

$$
v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_{k-1}
$$

are distinct, provided the chosen base point detects the kernel faithfully.

The natural faithful base is:

$$
z=(1,0).
$$

Acting on this state simply reveals the kernel itself:

$$
r\star(1,0)=r.
$$

Therefore:

$$
v_j=r^j.
$$

At this point DkMath already has the algebraic content of the vertex set of a regular polygon:

```text
one fixed boundary
one repeated transition
finite return
no early return
exactly k distinct orbit states
```

No edge, convex hull, interior, or visual polygon has yet been assumed.

## 7. The Euclidean reading comes later

The existing `EuclideanPhase` module already proves that a fixed real `q2` level can be interpreted as the standard Euclidean circle equation.

It also transports the core-zero action to the standard oriented rotation by:

$$
\frac\pi2.
$$

The new branch extends this from the quarter-turn case to an arbitrary real parameter.

For the real kernel:

$$
r_\theta=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),
$$

CF2D acts by:

$$
(x,y)\longmapsto
(\cos\theta\,x-\sin\theta\,y,
 \cos\theta\,y+\sin\theta\,x).
$$

After transport to the standard Euclidean plane, this should agree with Mathlib's oriented rotation by `theta`.

For:

$$
\theta=\frac{2\pi}{k},
$$

the algebraic finite orbit is then read as the ordinary equal-angle orbit on the unit circle.

This does not change the proof order.

```text
first:
  algebraic conserved orbit

later:
  Euclidean circle and angle interpretation
```

## 8. Where Gauss–Wantzel enters

For every positive `k`, the real trigonometric generator:

$$
r_k=\left(\cos\frac{2\pi}{k},\sin\frac{2\pi}{k}\right)
$$

returns after `k` products.

For suitable `k`, it has exact order `k`.

Therefore the orbit exists whether or not the regular `k`-gon is classically constructible.

The constructibility question is different:

> Can the coordinates of `r_k` be obtained from rational data by finitely many field operations and square-root extensions?

Classically, for `k\ge3`, the answer is yes exactly when:

$$
k=2^a p_1p_2\cdots p_s,
$$

where the `p_i` are distinct Fermat primes.

A Fermat number has the form:

$$
F_j=2^{2^j}+1.
$$

When it is prime, it is a Fermat prime.

Thus the classical theorem becomes a statement about the generator:

```text
The regular CF2D orbit exists for every positive k.

Its one-step unit kernel is quadratically constructible
exactly for Gauss–Wantzel indices.
```

This is the new DkMath interpretation of the theorem.

## 9. Why Euler's totient appears

Let:

$$
\zeta_k=e^{2\pi i/k}.
$$

The cyclotomic field generated by `ζ_k` has degree:

$$
[\mathbb Q(\zeta_k):\mathbb Q]=\varphi(k).
$$

Straightedge-and-compass constructions are governed by towers of quadratic extensions. Therefore the relevant field degree must be a power of two.

In the cyclotomic setting, the extension is Galois and abelian, so a power-of-two degree can be decomposed through a chain of index-two subgroups. This is why the degree criterion becomes sufficient here.

Hence the arithmetic bridge:

$$
\varphi(k)\text{ is a power of two}.
$$

Writing the odd part of `k` as a product of prime powers shows:

```text
an odd prime may occur only once;
for every odd prime q dividing k, q - 1 must be a power of two;
a prime of the form 2^m + 1 requires m to be a power of two;
therefore q is a Fermat prime.
```

This is the number-theoretic route that produces the Gauss–Wantzel form.

The DkMath implementation may reuse Mathlib's Euler-totient theory. GN5 should be used only where a concrete GN divisibility theorem genuinely matches an arithmetic obligation.

## 10. Why the project does not begin with geometric construction steps

A literal straightedge-and-compass formalization must define and control:

```text
lines through points
circles with centers and radii
line-line intersections
line-circle intersections
circle-circle intersections
non-degenerate cases
zero, one, or two intersection choices
finite construction histories
```

This is faithful to physical construction, but it creates a large geometric infrastructure before reaching the CF2D generator.

The planned v0 route starts algebraically.

A real coordinate is called quadratically constructible when it can be produced from rational constants by finitely many uses of:

```text
addition
subtraction
multiplication
nonzero inversion
nonnegative square root
```

The two coordinates of a unit kernel can then be tested independently.

This immediately supports the theorem:

```text
constructible generator
  -> every finite kernel power has constructible coordinates
  -> every orbit vertex is constructible
```

A later bridge can prove that this algebraic notion agrees with the intended geometric construction notion.

Until that bridge exists, theorem names should say `QuadraticallyConstructible` rather than using an unqualified geometric claim.

## 11. What the first branch can prove safely

The first branch has a strong and useful target even before the complete Gauss–Wantzel proof is finished.

It can prove:

```text
1. Repeating a KernelFamily parameter n times equals taking the nth kernel power.

2. The normalized scalar phase 1/k becomes a real unit kernel returning after k products.

3. Kernel return implies action return on every square-mass boundary.

4. The generated orbit remains on one fixed q2 boundary.

5. Exact order, once proved, gives k distinct states indexed by Fin k.

6. The orbit transports to the existing Euclidean model.

7. The Gauss–Wantzel integer form is represented as a precise arithmetic predicate.

8. Quadratic constructibility is separated from mere periodicity.
```

That is already a substantial theorem package.

## 12. The central contrast to preserve

The most important pair of examples is:

```text
k = 17
  the CF2D orbit closes
  the one-step generator is classically constructible

k = 7
  the CF2D orbit also closes
  the one-step generator is not classically constructible
```

The difference is not closure.

The difference is whether the one-step kernel can be generated by the permitted finite quadratic operations.

This contrast prevents a common conceptual error:

```text
closed orbit
  does not mean
constructible regular polygon
```

## 13. The 360-degree and 720-degree observation

CF2D records the action on the two-component state. When the state returns, CF2D sees a completed finite order.

A richer physical system may carry additional structure that has not returned yet. The familiar arm-and-book motion is an example: the visible book can complete one spatial turn while the full arm configuration needs another turn to return.

Mathematically, this is the distinction between a representation and a lift of that representation.

```text
ordinary planar vector state:
  one full turn returns

spinorial or lifted state:
  two full turns may be required
```

The present regular-orbit theorem concerns the CF2D vector representation. It should not claim to classify every lifted configuration space.

This is not a defect. It is an explicit statement of what the observer remembers.

The future design may compare:

```text
SO(2)-like boundary action
Spin(2)-like lifted phase
```

but that is outside the v0 Gauss–Wantzel connection.

## 14. The resulting theorem story

The complete long-term story is:

```text
A normalized cycle admits a scalar 1/k division.

The additive unit-kernel family turns scalar repetition into kernel multiplication.

The one-step kernel returns after k products.

Minimality gives exact order k.

The exact-order kernel generates k distinct states on one conserved q2 boundary.

The Euclidean bridge reads those states as equally rotated points on a circle.

Quadratic constructibility asks whether the one-step kernel can be built by finite square-root extensions.

The Gauss–Wantzel arithmetic condition classifies exactly those indices.
```

In one sentence:

> A regular polygon is first a constructible exact-order unit-kernel orbit; its visible Euclidean shape is the later interpretation.

That is the connection this branch is designed to formalize.
